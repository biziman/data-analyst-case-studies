Case 01

Mobile Game A: acquisition analytics

A mobile-game team must decide whether to shift acquisition spend across its eight campaigns (A–H) after one month of cost and player-activity data.
Assessment data · anonymized · real, not syntheticReviewed 2026-11-04Published 2026-11-06

What this page shows

The notebook compares eight acquisition campaigns on cost per day-7 payer, funnel rates, and revenue per registered player, then sizes a follow-up experiment. Every number below is computed from the four files in the repository — drag the controls and the answers change with them.

The data is real: records from a mobile-game hiring assessment, employer and game anonymized. This is a portfolio demonstration, not a client outcome.

Which campaigns clear the $50 screen?

CPP divides a campaign's May cost by its day-7 payers — a payer is a registered user with at least one payment within 168 hours of registration. The screen labels CPP ≤ $50 as meets target and CPP > $50 as above target.

Drag the threshold. Campaign membership, both pooled funnel charts, and the caption recompute together. At the default $50, three campaigns meet the screen: B at $40.00, E at $43.70, G at $44.80. The pooled gap in tutorial completion (53.85% vs 47.17%) describes the groups; it does not show that cheap campaigns produce better players.

Does the ranking hold across observation windows?

ARPU divides in-window payment revenue by registered users in the campaign, with non-payers kept at zero. Switch the window from 7 to 180 days: values move, and longer windows silently include registrants who never had a full follow-up period — which understates their ARPU.

There is also a definitional trap in the source notebook: two window rules disagree for the same metric (see the note under the chart). The rule, not the data, explains the difference — so any ARPU number in a report is meaningless without its window contract.

Campaign E vs H: what the test actually saw

Campaign E's strict 168-hour ARPU is $0.68; campaign H's is $0.29. A Mann–Whitney U test on user-level payment totals reports p ≈ 0.0007. Both distributions are skewed with a large mass of zeros — switch the scale and look at them before believing any single number.

Two caveats travel with that p-value: no effect size or confidence interval is reported, and the E/H pair was selected after inspecting the data. Treat it as a lead worth a clean experiment, not as a finding.

Design the next test

Campaign E's baseline day-3 payer conversion is 4.17%. The notebook sizes a follow-up experiment for a 20% relative improvement: about 7,764 users per arm for a one-sided test at α = 0.05 with 80% power — roughly 15.5 days at 1,000 daily registrations.

What this cannot show

  1. Campaign comparisons are observational. Nothing here shows that a campaign caused higher-value players.
  2. The day-7 payer filter accepts payments logged before registration, and cost dates are not aligned to registration cohorts.
  3. The two ARPU window rules disagree ($0.75 vs $0.68 for campaign E at 7 days), and longer windows include registrants without full follow-up.
  4. The cohort SQL in the notebook does not aggregate daily revenue before its cumulative sum and truncates elapsed days — its curve is not a validated LTV estimate.
  5. The E/H test reports a p-value without an effect size or confidence interval, and the pair was selected on the same data.
  6. The 6,686 registrations without a campaign value drop out of all campaign-level denominators.
  7. Publication permission for the assessment CSVs is not confirmed.
